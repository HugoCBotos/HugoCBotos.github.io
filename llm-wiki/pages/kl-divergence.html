5. KL Divergence

🎯 In one sentence

KL divergence measures how "far apart" two probability distributions are. It's the central quantity in variational inference — we find the distribution q that minimizes KL[q || p].

In BNNs, we have a true posterior p(w|D) that we can't compute, and we want to approximate it with a simpler distribution q(w) (usually a Gaussian). KL divergence tells us how bad our approximation is.

Definition

For two continuous distributions p and q over the same space:

KL Divergence
$$\operatorname{KL}[q \parallel p] = \int q(w) \log\frac{q(w)}{p(w)}\,dw = \mathbb{E}_{q}[\log q(w) - \log p(w)]$$

Key properties:

  • $\operatorname{KL}[q \parallel p] \ge 0$ always, with equality iff $q = p$ almost everywhere
  • Not symmetric: $\operatorname{KL}[q \parallel p] \neq \operatorname{KL}[p \parallel q]$ in general
  • Not a metric (doesn't satisfy triangle inequality), but behaves like a "distance"

Intuition

Intuition: KL[q || p] is the "information lost" when we use q to approximate p. Or: it's the number of extra bits of information needed to encode samples from p using a code optimized for q.

For our purposes, the most important interpretation is: $\operatorname{KL}[q \parallel p]$ is the penalty we pay for using $q$ instead of $p$. When $q$ puts probability mass where $p$ has none, the penalty is huge. When $q$ puts mass everywhere $p$ has mass (even if $q$ is wider), the penalty is small.

Mode-Seeking vs. Covering Behavior

$\operatorname{KL}[q \parallel p]$ is mode-seeking (also called "zero-avoiding"). To understand why, look at the formula again:

KL Divergence (integral form)
$$\operatorname{KL}[q \parallel p] = \int q(w) \log\frac{q(w)}{p(w)}\,dw$$

The integrand has two factors: $q(w)$ (the probability under $q$) multiplied by $\log(q(w)/p(w))$ (how much $q$ and $p$ differ at $w$). Let's examine two scenarios:

ScenarioWhat happens in the integralPenalty
$q$ puts mass where $p$ has none
($q$ is wider than $p$, or $q$ is centered somewhere $p$ isn't)		 $q(w)$ is large, $p(w) \approx 0$, so $q(w)/p(w) \to \infty$,
$\log(\infty) \to \infty$ — the integrand explodes		 HUGE PENALTY
$q$ puts no mass where $p$ has mass
($q$ is narrower than $p$, or $q$ misses a mode of $p$)		 $q(w) \approx 0$, so $q(w) \cdot \log(\dots) \approx 0 \cdot \text{anything} = 0$,
regardless of what $p(w)$ is		 ZERO PENALTY

Analogy: Imagine two photographers shooting a mountain range. The true landscape is $p$ (the mountain range). You are $q$ (the photographer). You can choose your lens:

  • Narrow lens ($q$ is too narrow): Misses distant peaks entirely, but the penalty is small — you're penalized only for what you actually capture ($q(w) \neq 0$), not for what you miss.
  • Wide lens ($q$ is too wide): Includes empty sky around the edges. The penalty is huge — trying to capture areas where there's no mountain ($p(w) \approx 0$) costs you dearly.

That's why minimizing $\operatorname{KL}[q \parallel p]$ produces a narrow $q$ that covers one mode well: it's cheaper to be narrow and miss parts of $p$ than to be wide and include areas where $p$ is small.

The "Zero-Avoiding" Property

$\operatorname{KL}[q \parallel p]$ is called "zero-avoiding" because $q$ absolutely must not have mass where $p$ has none — that's catastrophically expensive. But $q$ is allowed to be zero where $p$ has mass — that costs nothing. This is entirely due to the $q(w)$ factor outside the log: where $q(w) = 0$, the whole term is zero regardless of $p(w)$.

The asymmetry is the opposite for $\operatorname{KL}[p \parallel q]$ (the reverse direction). There, the roles are swapped: $p(w)$ multiplies the log, so $p$ being large where $q$ is small would be penalized. That's why $\operatorname{KL}[p \parallel q]$ is "mass-covering" or "mean-seeking" — it tries to make $q$ cover all of $p$.

Consequence for Bayesian Neural Networks

$\operatorname{KL}[q \parallel p(w \mid D)]$ minimization → $q$ is too narrow → overconfident predictions

In variational inference (Bayes by Backprop), we minimize $\operatorname{KL}[q \parallel p(w \mid D)]$ where $q$ is our Gaussian approximation and $p(w \mid D)$ is the true (complex, multi-modal) posterior.

  • $q$ finds one mode of the true posterior and fits itself to that mode.
  • $q$ is typically narrower than the true posterior around that mode (because being wider would include low-probability regions = expensive).
  • $q$ ignores other modes entirely (no penalty for missing them).
  • Result: the variational approximation underestimates uncertainty — it's overconfident. This is a well-known limitation of mean-field VI for BNNs.

This is exactly what Izmailov et al. (2021) confirmed empirically: mean-field VI produces posteriors that are too narrow compared to the gold standard HMC. The next section on variational inference shows how this plays out in practice.

Computing KL Divergence in PyTorch

For two Gaussians, there's a closed-form formula:

import torch

def kl_gaussian(mu_q, sigma_q, mu_p, sigma_p):
    """
    KL[q || p] for 1D Gaussians q = N(mu_q, sigma_q^2), p = N(mu_p, sigma_p^2)
    Formula: KL[q||p] = log(σ_p/σ_q) + (σ_q² + (μ_q-μ_p)²) / (2σ_p²) - 1/2
    """
    return (
        torch.log(sigma_p / sigma_q) +
        (sigma_q**2 + (mu_q - mu_p)**2) / (2 * sigma_p**2) -
        0.5
    )

# Example: q = N(0, 1), p = N(0, 2)  (q is narrower)
kl = kl_gaussian(0.0, 1.0, 0.0, 2.0)
print(kl)  # → about 0.35 (small: q is inside p)

# Example: q = N(0, 2), p = N(0, 1)  (q is wider)
kl = kl_gaussian(0.0, 2.0, 0.0, 1.0)
print(kl)  # → about 0.81 (larger: q has mass where p doesn't)

PyTorch also has a built-in KL divergence class for Gaussians (useful when working with distributions): 

import torch.distributions as dist

q = dist.Normal(0.0, 1.0)
p = dist.Normal(0.0, 2.0)
kl = dist.kl_divergence(q, p)
print(kl)  # → 0.35
🔗 Connection: KL divergence is the second term in the ELBO (next page). The first term (data fit) pushes q to explain the data; the KL term pushes q to stay close to the prior. The balance between them determines the posterior approximation.
