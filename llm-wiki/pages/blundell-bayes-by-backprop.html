Paper Index / Bayes by Backprop

Bayes by Backprop

Weight Uncertainty in Neural Networks — Blundell, Cornebise, Kavukcuoglu, Wierstra (2015)

📄 Paper: arXiv:1505.05424
📁 Source: 2015_Blundell_Bayes_by_Backprop.pdf
🎯 Core Idea

Instead of learning one fixed number per weight (as in standard neural networks), learn two numbers per weight: a best guess (mean), and a range of uncertainty (spread). Then, when making predictions, randomly wiggle the weights according to their spreads and average the results — you get an answer plus a confidence interval.

What Problem Does This Solve?

In a standard neural network, each weight $w$ is a single number. Training finds the single best value for each weight. This gives good predictions on average, but the network has no way of telling you when it's uncertain — it always gives one answer, even when that answer is a wild guess.

Bayes by Backprop says: instead of learning one number per weight, learn a Gaussian distribution for each weight. A Gaussian is defined by two numbers: a mean $\mu$ (the best guess) and a standard deviation $\sigma$ (the spread). When we want to use the network, we:

  1. Sample random weights from each Gaussian — this gives one "virtual network"
  2. Run a forward pass through this virtual network
  3. Repeat $T$ times with different random weight samples
  4. Average the $T$ outputs → this is your prediction
  5. Take the variance of the $T$ outputs → this is your uncertainty estimate

If the $T$ outputs agree closely, the network is confident. If they disagree wildly, the network is saying "I have no idea."

How to Train: The ELBO

Training means finding the right $\mu$ and $\sigma$ for every weight. The objective function has two competing goals:

$$\text{ELBO} = \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{q}[\log p(D \mid w)]}_{\text{make good predictions}} - \underbrace{\text{KL}[q(w) \parallel p(w)]}_{\text{keep weights small}}$$
  • Make good predictions (first term): The network's predictions should match the training data. This is the same goal as standard training.
  • Keep weights small (second term): Regularization — a penalty term that discourages the weight distributions from becoming too spread out. This prevents overfitting and keeps the model simple unless the data strongly demands complexity.

The name "ELBO" just means this is a lower bound on something we'd really like to compute (the evidence), but can't. Maximizing the ELBO is the best we can do.

The Tricky Part: Making Randomness Differentiable

Here's the technical difficulty: we need to compute gradients (derivatives) of the loss with respect to $\mu$ and $\sigma$. But the network's output depends on random samples from $\mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)$ — and you can't differentiate through a random number generator. If you try, the gradient is noise.

The reparameterization trick solves this with a simple math trick:

$$w = \mu + \sigma \cdot \varepsilon, \quad \text{where } \varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$$

Instead of sampling $w$ directly (which blocks gradients), sample a standard random number $\varepsilon$ from $\mathcal{N}(0, 1)$ (which doesn't depend on $\mu$ or $\sigma$), then scale and shift it. Now the gradient can flow through $\mu$ and $\sigma$ — they're just deterministic operations.

Noise Reduction: Sampling Activations Instead of Weights

The reparameterization trick lets gradients flow, but the estimates can be noisy — especially when you have millions of weights and each one gets its own random sample. The noise in the gradients makes training slow and unstable.

A follow-up paper (Kingma, Salimans & Welling 2015) noticed something clever: instead of adding randomness to each weight individually, you can add randomness to the pre-activation (the weighted sum before the activation function). For a layer $b = w a$ (where $w$ is Gaussian-distributed and $a$ is the input):

$$b \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu a,\; \sigma^2 a^2)$$

This single random sample replaces millions of individual weight samples. Less randomness → less gradient noise → faster, more stable training. This is called the local reparameterization trick. It's not in Blundell's original paper, but it's commonly used together with Bayes by Backprop in practice.

Algorithm Summary (Plain English)

  1. To make a prediction with current $\mu, \sigma$: sample random weights $w = \mu + \sigma \cdot \varepsilon$, run the network, record the output. Repeat $T$ times. Average = prediction, variance = uncertainty.
  2. To train (update $\mu, \sigma$):
    1. Sample random weights using reparameterization (or the local version for efficiency).
    2. Compute the loss: prediction error + the KL penalty (which is just a formula involving $\mu$, $\sigma$, and the prior).
    3. Backpropagate gradients to $\mu$ and $\sigma$ — this works because of the reparameterization trick.
    4. Update $\mu$ and $\sigma$ with a standard optimizer (Adam, SGD).

Practical Notes

  • Ensuring $\sigma > 0$: Standard deviations must be positive numbers. Instead of learning $\sigma$ directly (which could go negative), learn $\rho$ (any real number) and compute $\sigma = \log(1 + e^\rho)$. This is called the "softplus" transformation and it's always positive.
  • What prior to use? A single Gaussian prior tends to make all weights too similar. Blundell uses a mixture of two Gaussians — one with small spread (encourages tiny weights, useful for pruning) and one with large spread (allows important weights to grow).
  • Cost: $2\times$ the parameters of a standard network (every weight has a $\mu$ and a $\sigma$), and about $2\times$ slower per forward pass (due to sampling).
  • KL annealing: The KL penalty can overwhelm the prediction term early in training. Gradually increasing its weight (starting from 0) often helps.

Pros & Cons for NN Practitioners

✅ Advantages❌ Disadvantages
Principled Bayesian inference with uncertainty estimates Gaussian posterior assumption is restrictive — real posteriors are often multi-modal and heavy-tailed
Works with any architecture (MLP, CNN, RNN) 2× parameter blowup can be significant for large models
Standard backpropagation — easy to implement in PyTorch/TF Gradient estimates can still be high-variance for deep networks
Provides both epistemic and aleatoric uncertainty Diagonal covariance posterior ignores weight correlations
🔗 Connections: This paper is the foundation for most modern VI-based BNN work. Louizos & Welling (2017) extended it with normalizing flows for richer posteriors. Izmailov et al. (2021) showed that the Gaussian posterior assumption is often severely misspecified — consider this when interpreting uncertainty.
