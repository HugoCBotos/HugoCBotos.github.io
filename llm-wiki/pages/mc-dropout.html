8. Monte Carlo Dropout

🎯 In one sentence

If you train a network with dropout + L2 weight decay, you are already doing variational inference — the ELBO is hidden inside the standard training loss. MC Dropout just reveals the Bayesian interpretation at test time.

⏰ When does the Bayesian part happen? Training vs. Inference

This is a common point of confusion, so let's compare the three main methods side by side:

MethodTrainingInference (test time)
Bayes by Backprop Learns a distribution (μ, σ) for each weight — training itself is Bayesian Sample from the learned distribution, average predictions
MC Dropout Standard training with dropout + L2 — secretly = ELBO, but you don't need to know that to train Keep dropout on, run T forward passes, average — the Bayesian part is here
Laplace Completely standard MAP training — no Bayesian component at all After training: compute Hessian, fit Gaussian, sample — purely post-hoc

MC Dropout is unusual: it affects training (dropout is a regularizer), but the Bayesian interpretation only becomes visible at test time. That's why it's confusing — you train a normal network, then suddenly at test time you get uncertainty estimates.

⚠️ Your confusion is justified

If you read pages 1-6 and then jumped here, you're thinking: "Wait — I spent all that time learning about priors, posteriors, KL divergence, and ELBO... and now this method just says 'train normally'? Where's the Bayesian part?"

The answer is subtle: the Bayesian part was hidden inside the standard training all along. Gal & Ghahramani (2016) showed that dropout training + L2 weight decay is mathematically equivalent to optimizing a valid ELBO. The discovery was that a popular regularization trick (dropout) was secretly doing Bayesian inference — nobody knew this until 2016.

The Key Insight: Dropout Training = ELBO Optimization

Let's compare what we do in standard dropout training vs. what variational inference does:

Variational Inference (page 7)Dropout Training
What we optimize $\operatorname{ELBO} = \mathbb{E}_{q}[\log p(D \mid w)] - \operatorname{KL}[q \parallel p(w)]$ Loss = cross-entropy + λ · ||w||2 (L2 weight decay)
Posterior q(w) $q(w) = \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)$ $q(w) = w \odot z$, $z_i \sim \text{Bernoulli}(1-p)$
Prior p(w) $p(w) = \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_p^2)$ Same! The L2 term λ||w||2 comes from a Gaussian prior
Data term $\mathbb{E}_{q}[\log p(D \mid w)]$ Cross-entropy loss (with dropout = MC estimate of the expectation)

Equivalence (Gal & Ghahramani, 2016):

$$\text{CrossEntropy}(\hat{y}, y) + \lambda \,\|w\|^2 \;\Longleftrightarrow\; -\mathbb{E}_{q}[\log p(D \mid w)] + \frac{\operatorname{KL}[q \parallel p(w)]}{N}$$

Where N is the number of training points. The L2 weight decay λ is determined by the prior variance. The dropout rate p controls the "shape" of the variational posterior q.

In other words: every time you trained a neural network with dropout and L2 regularization, you were maximizing an ELBO without knowing it. The ELBO was hiding in your loss function. The cross-entropy evaluates the data fit (first term), and the L2 weight decay evaluates the KL divergence against a Gaussian prior (second term).

What is Dropout?

Dropout is a regularization technique. During training, randomly "drop" (set to zero) a fraction p of the neurons in each layer. This prevents the network from relying too much on any single neuron.

Dropout
$$\text{During training: } h_{\text{out}} = h_{\text{in}} \odot z, \quad z_i \sim \text{Bernoulli}(1-p)$$

The key Bayesian interpretation: the random mask z defines a variational posterior over the weights:

📝 What is wMAP?

MAP stands for Maximum A Posteriori — it's the single "best" weight value that maximizes the posterior probability p(w|D).

This is exactly what normal neural network training produces. When you train with cross-entropy loss + L2 weight decay, the final weights are the MAP estimate. The L2 regularization comes from the prior, and the cross-entropy comes from the likelihood — together they define the posterior.

So wMAP is just the ordinary trained weights you get from optimizer.step() after many epochs. No new concept needed.

Variational posterior implied by dropout
$$q(w) \propto z \odot w_{\text{MAP}}, \quad z_i \sim \text{Bernoulli}(1-p)$$

The MC Dropout Algorithm

Once we understand that dropout training = ELBO optimization, the "MC" part is just Monte Carlo estimation of the predictive distribution:

Predictive distribution via MC Dropout
$$p(y^* \mid x^*, D) \approx \frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^{T} p(y^* \mid x^*, w_t)$$
where $w_t$ are weights with different dropout masks applied. This is the same formula as in Bayes by Backprop, but with Bernoulli noise instead of Gaussian noise on the weights.
  1. Train your network with dropout and L2 regularization (this is maximizing the ELBO).
  2. At test time, set the model to train mode so dropout stays active.
  3. Run the same input through the network T times — each pass has a different random dropout mask, which is like drawing a different sample from q(w).
  4. Predictive mean: average of the T outputs.
  5. Predictive uncertainty: variance of the T outputs.

Summary of the logic chain:

  1. During training: cross-entropy + L2 + dropout  =  maximizing an ELBO
  2. Therefore: the trained network's weights + dropout define a variational posterior q(w)
  3. At test time: keep dropout on to sample from q(w), average predictions to get the Bayesian predictive distribution
  4. The variance across samples = epistemic uncertainty (model uncertainty)

PyTorch Implementation

Here is the simplest possible MC Dropout implementation. You only need to change how you test — training is exactly the same as a normal network:

import torch
import torch.nn as nn
import torch.nn.functional as F

# ---------- Step 1: Define a network with dropout ----------
# (This is the SAME as any normal network with dropout)
class NetWithDropout(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self):
        super().__init__()
        self.fc1 = nn.Linear(784, 256)
        self.fc2 = nn.Linear(256, 128)
        self.fc3 = nn.Linear(128, 10)
        self.dropout = nn.Dropout(p=0.5)
    
    def forward(self, x):
        x = F.relu(self.fc1(x))
        x = self.dropout(x)         # ← this is the variational posterior sampling!
        x = F.relu(self.fc2(x))
        x = self.dropout(x)         # ← each forward pass = sample from q(w)
        x = self.fc3(x)
        return x

# ---------- Step 2: Train normally (this IS maximizing the ELBO!) ----------
model = NetWithDropout()
optimizer = torch.optim.Adam(model.parameters(), lr=0.001,
                             weight_decay=1e-4)  # ← L2 = KL term in the ELBO

for x_batch, y_batch in train_loader:
    pred = model(x_batch)
    loss = F.cross_entropy(pred, y_batch)  # ← data fit term in the ELBO
    
    optimizer.zero_grad()
    loss.backward()
    optimizer.step()

# ---------- Step 3: Test with MC Dropout ----------
model.train()  # ← KEEP DROPOUT ACTIVE to sample from q(w)

T = 50
all_preds = []

with torch.no_grad():
    for _ in range(T):
        pred = F.softmax(model(x_test), dim=1)   # each pass = different dropout mask
        all_preds.append(pred)                    #           = different w ~ q(w)

all_preds = torch.stack(all_preds)        # (T, batch, 10)
mean_pred = all_preds.mean(dim=0)         # Bayesian predictive mean
uncertainty = all_preds.var(dim=0)        # Epistemic uncertainty
💡 The key takeaway: MC Dropout is deceptively simple on the surface, but underneath it's doing the same thing as Bayes by Backprop: maximizing an ELBO during training and Monte Carlo averaging during testing. The only difference is the shape of the variational posterior: Bernoulli (on/off) instead of Gaussian. That's why it's called "Bayesian" — it literally is variational inference, just with a different posterior family.

How Many Forward Passes?

TQualitySpeed
10Rough estimate10× test time
50Good50× test time
100Very good100× test time
1000ExcellentUsually not worth it

In practice, T = 30–50 gives reliable uncertainty estimates for most problems. The variance of the estimate decreases as 1/√T.

Choosing the Dropout Rate

The dropout rate p controls the quality of the uncertainty:

  • Low dropout (p ≈ 0.1): Low uncertainty, may be overconfident.
  • High dropout (p ≈ 0.5): Higher uncertainty, better calibrated.
  • Too high (p > 0.8): Too much noise, hurts accuracy.

Concrete Dropout (Gal et al., 2017) is an extension that learns the dropout rate automatically by gradient descent. The idea: treat p as a learnable parameter and optimize it along with the weights.

Pros and Cons

✅ Advantages❌ Disadvantages
Zero extra training cost Only captures epistemic uncertainty (partially)
Trivial to implement (5 lines of code) Bernoulli approximation is crude
Works with any existing trained model Dropout rate needs manual tuning
Surprisingly well-calibrated in practice T× slower at test time
⚡ Bottom line: MC Dropout is the first thing to try when you need uncertainty estimates from a neural network. It's simple, it's free (training-wise), and it often works surprisingly well. If you need better uncertainty, then try Bayes by Backprop or Laplace.
