Complex sequences

A sequence of complex numbers zn=an+ibnz_n = a_n+ib_n converges to z=a+ibz=a+ib if an→aa_n \to a and bn→bb_n \to b. We denote this by zn→z.z_n \to z. Equivalently, zn→zz_n \to z if |z−zn|=(an−a)2+(bn−b)2→0.|z-z_n| = \sqrt{(a_n-a)^2+(b_n-b)^2} \to 0. On the other hand, znz_n diverges to infinity, zn→∞z_n \to \infty, if |zn|→∞|z_n| \to \infty.

For instance, n+inn2+ni→0\frac {n+i\sqrt n} {n^2+n i} \to 0. We can see this in two ways. Brute force: n+inn2+ni=(n+in)(n2−in)n4+n2=n3+n3/2n4+n2+in5/2−n2n4+n2=1n+1n5/21+1n2+i1n3/2−1n21+1n2→0\frac {n+i\sqrt n} {n^2+n i} = \frac{(n+i\sqrt n)(n^2 - in)}{n^4+n^2}= \frac{n^3+n^{3/2}}{n^4+n^2}+i\frac{n^{5/2}-n^2}{n^4+n^2} = \frac{\frac1n+\frac1{n^{5/2}}}{1+\frac1{n^2}}+i\frac{\frac1{n^{3/2}}-\frac 1{n^2}}{1+\frac 1{n^2}} \to 0 Using the norm: |n+inn2+ni|=n2+nn4+n2=1n1+1n1+1n2→0.\left\vert\frac {n+i\sqrt n} {n^2+n i} \right\vert= \frac{\sqrt{n^2+n}}{\sqrt{n^4+n^2}}= \frac1n\frac{\sqrt{1+\frac 1n}}{\sqrt{1+\frac1{n^2}}} \to 0.

The rules for limits of sums, products, and conjugates follow from the corresponding rules for real sequences. Indeed, addition and multiplication express the real and imaginary parts as sums and products of real sequences, while conjugation changes an+ibna_n+ib_n to an−ibna_n-ib_n.

Similarly, znz_n is a Cauchy sequence if |zn−zm|→0|z_n-z_m| \to 0, which is the same as saying that ana_n and bnb_n are Cauchy sequences. Since the real numbers are complete, both ana_n and bnb_n converge. Hence znz_n converges, so the complex numbers are also complete.

Now, we define the exponential of complex numbers.

Theorem: The sequence qn=(1+zn)nq_n = \left(1+\frac zn\right)^n is a Cauchy sequence. Its limit is denoted by eze^z.

Proof:

By the binomial theorem, (1+zn)n=1+z+∑k=2nzkk!(1−1n)⋯(1−k−1n).\left(1+\frac zn\right)^n =1+z+\sum_{k=2}^{n}\frac{z^k}{k!} \left(1-\frac1n\right)\cdots\left(1-\frac{k-1}{n}\right). Take m>nm>n. For 2≤k≤n2\leq k\leq n, each factor in the coefficient of zkz^k satisfies 0≤1−jn≤1−jm(1≤j≤k−1).0\leq1-\frac jn\leq1-\frac jm \qquad (1\leq j\leq k-1). Multiplying these inequalities shows that the coefficient of zkz^k in (1+zm)m\left(1+\frac zm\right)^m is at least as large as its coefficient in (1+zn)n\left(1+\frac zn\right)^n. The terms of degrees n+1,…,mn+1,\ldots,m occur only in the first polynomial and also have positive coefficients. The constant and linear terms cancel.

Thus, we can write qm−qn=b2z2+⋯+bmzm,q_m-q_n=b_2z^2+\cdots+b_mz^m, where every bk≥0b_k\geq0. By the triangle inequality, |qm−qn|≤b2|z|2+⋯+bm|z|m.|q_m-q_n|\leq b_2|z|^2+\cdots+b_m|z|^m. The sum on the right is exactly the same polynomial difference evaluated at |z||z| instead of zz. Therefore, |qm−qn|≤(1+|z|m)m−(1+|z|n)n.|q_m-q_n| \leq\left(1+\frac{|z|}{m}\right)^m -\left(1+\frac{|z|}{n}\right)^n. We already know that the real sequence (1+|z|n)n\left(1+\frac{|z|}{n}\right)^n converges to e|z|e^{|z|}. Consequently, the right-hand side tends to zero as n,m→∞n,m\to\infty. The case n>mn>m follows by interchanging the indices. Hence qnq_n is a Cauchy sequence. QED

Note that ez¯=ez¯\overline{e^z} = e^{\overline z}. Indeed, (1+zn)n¯=(1+z¯n)n,\overline{\left(1+\frac zn\right)^n} =\left(1+\frac{\overline z}{n}\right)^n, and taking the limit gives the identity.

Additionally, writing z=a+ibz = a+ib, we have ez=eaeib.e^z = e^ae^{ib}. The number eae^a is a real exponential. The interesting thing is that eibe^{ib} is a point on the unit circle in the complex plane: |eib|2=eibeib¯=eibe−ib=e0=1.\left|e^{ib}\right|^2 = e^{ib} \overline{e^{ib}} = e^{ib}e^{-ib} = e^{0}=1.

So, in order to understand the exponential of complex numbers, we just need to understand eiθe^{i\theta} with θ\theta real.
