Power series

In this section we will discuss functions that can be written
a0+a1(z−s)+a2(z−s)2+a3(z−s)3+⋯a_0 +a_1(z-s)+a_2(z-s)^2+a_3(z-s)^3+\cdots Think of it as an infinite degree polynomial.

These kinds of functions are called power series. In order to understand them, we need two important results on convergence of series. The first is Theorem 6.1, on absolutely convergent series, and the second is Theorem 7.1.

Conditionally convergent series

The series is conditionally convergent when |a0|+|a1|+|a2|+|a3|+⋯=∞anda0+a1+a2+a3+⋯converges.|a_0|+|a_1|+|a_2|+|a_3|+\cdots = \infty \quad\text {and} \quad a_0+a_1+a_2+a_3+\cdots \quad\text{converges}.These conditionally convergent series are horrible to work with because rearranging their terms can change their limits.

Example

The series 1−12+13−14+15−16+⋯1-\frac 12+\frac 13 -\frac 14+ \frac 15-\frac 16+\cdots is conditionally convergent. First of all, it is convergent due to Theorem 4.3 and its limit is log⁡(2)\log(2), a fact we will prove later. We need to show now that the harmonic sum Hn=11+12+13+14+⋯+1nH_n = \frac 1 1 +\frac 1 2+\frac 13+ \frac 14+\cdots +\frac 1 n diverges.

For n>1n>1 we observe the following phenomenon: 13+14>14+14=12,15+16+17+18>18+18+18+18=12,⋮12n−1+1+12n−1+2+⋯+12n>12n+12n+⋯+12n=12. \begin{aligned} \frac13+\frac14 &>\frac14+\frac14=\frac12,\\ \frac15+\frac16+\frac17+\frac18 &>\frac18+\frac18+\frac18+\frac18=\frac12,\\ &\vdots\\ \frac 1{2^{n-1}+1}+\frac 1{2^{n-1}+2}+\cdots+\frac 1{2^{n}} &>\frac 1{2^{n}}+\frac 1{2^{n}}+\cdots+\frac 1{2^{n}}=\frac12. \end{aligned} Therefore, H2n=1+12+13+⋯+12n≥1+n2→∞.H_{2^n}=1+\frac 12+\frac 13+\cdots+\frac1{2^n} \geq 1+\frac n2\to\infty.

In general, we can take lnl_n to be the largest integer satisfying 2ln≤n2^{l_n} \leq n. It is clear that 2ln≤n<2ln+12^{l_n}\leq n < 2^{l_n+1}. Therefore, ln≤log⁡2(n)<ln+1l_n \leq \log_2(n) < l_n+1, and Hn=1+12+13+⋯+1n≥1+12+13+⋯+12ln≥1+ln2→∞.\begin{align}H_n&=1+\frac12+\frac13+\cdots+\frac 1n \\&\geq 1+\frac 12+\frac 13 + \cdots+ \frac1{2^{l_n}} \\&\geq1 +\frac {l_n}2 \to \infty. \end{align}

Now concerning the rearrangement:

We take the positive terms 1,13,15,…1,\frac13,\frac15,\ldots in order, placing the next two unused negative terms after each one. This gives the groups below. In each group, the first two terms combine to half of the positive term: Tn=(1−12−14)+(13−16−18)+(15−110−112)+⋯+(12n+1−14n+2−14n+4)=(12−14)+(16−18)+(110−112)+⋯+(14n+2−14n+4)=12(1−12)+12(13−14)+12(15−16)+⋯+12(12n+1−12n+2)=12(1−12+13−14+15−16+⋯+12n+1−12n+2)→log⁡(2)2. \begin{aligned} T_n&=\left(1-\frac12-\frac14\right)+\left(\frac13-\frac16-\frac18\right)+\left(\frac15-\frac1{10}-\frac1{12}\right)+\cdots+\left(\frac1{2n+1}-\frac1{4n+2}-\frac1{4n+4}\right)\\ &=\left(\frac12-\frac14\right)+\left(\frac16-\frac18\right)+\left(\frac1{10}-\frac1{12}\right)+\cdots+\left(\frac1{4n+2}-\frac1{4n+4}\right)\\ &=\frac12\left(1-\frac12\right)+\frac12\left(\frac13-\frac14\right)+\frac12\left(\frac15-\frac16\right)+\cdots+\frac12\left(\frac1{2n+1}-\frac1{2n+2}\right)\\ &=\frac12\left(1-\frac12+\frac13-\frac14+\frac15-\frac16+\cdots+\frac1{2n+1}-\frac1{2n+2}\right) \to \frac {\log(2)}2. \end{aligned} To summarize, 1−12+13−14+15−16+⋯=log⁡(2)1-\frac12+\frac13-\frac14+\frac15-\frac16+\cdots = \log(2) and (1−12−14)+(13−16−18)+(15−110−112)+⋯=log⁡(2)2\left(1-\frac12-\frac14\right)+\left(\frac13-\frac16-\frac18\right)+\left(\frac15-\frac1{10}-\frac1{12}\right)+\cdots = \frac{\log(2)}2 have different values despite both summing the same coefficients. The difference is that the second series was obtained from the first by rearranging infinitely many terms. That is why conditional series are tricky, their values change when we change the way we sum them up.

The study of this strange phenomenon in the series above is due to the French mathematician Augustin-Louis Cauchy.

This weird behavior due to rearranging the coefficients goes much further. The Riemann rearrangement theorem, due to the German mathematician Bernhard Riemann in the 19th century, states that we can rearrange a conditionally convergent series of real numbers to give us any real number we want, or to diverge to +∞+\infty or −∞-\infty.

Absolutely convergent series

Theorem 6.1: Consider a sequence ak∈ℂa_k \in \mathbb C. If |a0|+|a1|+|a2|+|a3|+⋯<∞,|a_0|+|a_1|+|a_2|+|a_3|+\cdots< \infty,

meaning that this infinite sum is convergent, then the series a0+a1+a2+a3+⋯a_0+a_1+a_2+a_3+\cdots converges. In this situation, we say this series is absolutely convergent. Additionally, an absolutely convergent series does not change its limit when we rearrange its terms.

Proof:

First we show that a0+a1+a2+a3+⋯a_0+a_1+a_2+a_3+\cdots converges by showing that its partial sums form a Cauchy sequence. Define Sn=a0+a1+⋯+anS_n = a_0+a_1+\cdots+a_n and An=|a0|+|a1|+⋯+|an|A_n=|a_0|+|a_1|+\cdots+|a_n|. Consider n>mn>m. Just notice that Sn−SmS_n-S_m is the sum of the terms aia_i with m<i≤nm<i\leq n. Therefore, |Sn−Sm|≤An−Am.|S_n - S_m| \leq A_n - A_m. If m>nm>n, then |Sm−Sn|≤Am−An.|S_m-S_n| \leq A_m - A_n. In any case, |Sn−Sm|≤|An−Am|→0,|S_n-S_m| \leq |A_n-A_m| \to 0, because AnA_n converges.

Thus, SnS_n is a Cauchy sequence and consequently converges to some value SS.

Now we prove the limit of the series does not change under rearrangements. Consider a bijection σ:ℤ≥0→ℤ≥0\sigma: \mathbb Z_{\geq 0} \to \mathbb Z_{\geq 0} and define bi=aσ(i)b_i = a_{\sigma(i)}. Fix r>0r>0. There exists N∈ℤ≥0N \in \mathbb Z_{\geq 0} such that |aN+1|+|aN+2|+|aN+3|+⋯<rand|SN−S|<r.|a_{N+1}|+|a_{N+2}|+|a_{N+3}|+\cdots <r \quad \text{and} \quad |S_N - S| <r. Define Tn=b0+b1+b2+⋯+bn.T_n = b_0+b_1+b_2+\cdots+b_n.For some N′N' sufficiently large, the list b0,…,bN′b_0,\ldots,b_{N'} contains all the terms of a0,…,aNa_0,\ldots, a_N. Therefore, for n>N′n>N' we have |Tn−S|≤|Tn−SN|+|SN−S|≤(|aN+1|+|aN+2|+|aN+3|+⋯)+|SN−S|<2r.\begin{align}|T_n-S| &\leq |T_n-S_N|+|S_N-S|\\&\leq \left(|a_{N+1}|+|a_{N+2}|+|a_{N+3}|+\cdots\right)+ |S_N-S|<2r.\end{align} Therefore, Tn→ST_n \to S.

QED

We usually prove that a series is absolutely convergent using Theorem 6.2 below. The idea is to compare the tail |aN|+|aN+1|+|aN+2|+|aN+3|+⋯|a_N|+|a_{N+1}|+|a_{N+2}|+|a_{N+3}|+\cdots of the series |a0|+|a1|+|a2|+|a3|+⋯|a_0|+|a_1|+|a_2|+|a_3|+\cdotsfor some NN with a geometric series, with s>0s>0, 1+s+s2+s3+⋯ is{∞for s≥111−sfor s<11+s+s^2+s^3+\cdots \text \quad{is}\quad \begin{cases} \infty \quad \text{for }s\geq1\\ \frac 1{1-s} \quad \text{for }s<1\\ \end{cases} As a matter of fact, most of the important results in series follow from comparing one unknown series with a geometric one.

Ratio test and power series

Theorem 6.2 (ratio test): Assume that the ratio |ak+1||ak|\frac{|a_{k+1}|}{|a_k|} is well defined and converges to some limit LL. The series

There are other similar tests to the one above, like root test or limsup test, but I won't bother talking about them.

The exp⁡(z)\exp(z), sin⁡(θ)\sin(\theta), and cos⁡(θ)\cos(\theta) series converge for any zz or θ\theta. At zero, convergence is immediate; otherwise, we use Theorem 6.2. For the exponential we have ak=zkk!a_k = \frac{z^k}{k!}. Thus, |ak+1||ak|=|z|k+1→0.\frac{|a_{k+1}|}{|a_k|} = \frac{|z|}{k+1} \to 0. For the sine function ak=(−1)kθ2k+1(2k+1)!a_k = \frac{(-1)^k\theta^{2k+1}}{(2k+1)!} |ak+1||ak|=|θ|2(2k+3)(2k+2)→0.\frac{|a_{k+1}|}{|a_k|} = \frac{|\theta|^2}{(2k+3)(2k+2)} \to 0. For the cosine function ak=(−1)kθ2k(2k)!a_k = \frac{(-1)^k\theta^{2k}}{(2k)!} |ak+1||ak|=|θ|2(2k+2)(2k+1)→0.\frac{|a_{k+1}|}{|a_k|} = \frac{|\theta|^2}{(2k+2)(2k+1)} \to 0. Therefore, ez=1+z+z22!+z33!+z44!+⋯,e^z = 1+z+\frac{z^2}{2!}+\frac{z^3}{3!}+\frac{z^4}{4!}+\cdots, sin⁡(θ)=θ−θ33!+θ55!−θ77!+⋯,\sin(\theta) = \theta-\frac{\theta^3}{3!}+\frac{\theta^5}{5!}-\frac{\theta^7}{7!}+\cdots, cos⁡(θ)=1−θ22!+θ44!−θ66!+⋯\cos(\theta) = 1-\frac{\theta^2}{2!}+\frac{\theta^4}{4!}-\frac{\theta^6}{6!}+\cdots are absolutely convergent series.

As we said before functions of the form a0+a1(z−s)+a2(z−s)2+a3(z−s)3+⋯a_0 +a_1(z-s)+a_2(z-s)^2+a_3(z-s)^3+\cdots are called power series. So, the exponential, sine, and cosine power series converge for all zz. Here I want to point out that for sin\sin and cos\cos we have been considering θ\theta real, but nothing forbids us to define these functions for complex inputs via the power series. So, sin\sin and cos\cos are in fact complex valued functions as well.

Another example is f(z)=(z−1)−(z−1)22+(z−1)33−(z−1)44+⋯,f(z) = (z-1)-\frac{(z-1)^2}{2}+\frac{(z-1)^3}{3}-\frac{(z-1)^4}{4} + \cdots, this function converges absolutely for |z−1|<1|z-1|<1 and diverges for |z−1|>1|z-1|>1. The case z=1z=1 is immediate. For z≠1z\neq1, |z−1|k+2/(k+2)|z−1|k+1/(k+1)=|z−1|k+1k+2→|z−1|.\frac{|z-1|^{k+2}/(k+2)}{|z-1|^{k+1}/(k+1)} = |z-1| \frac{k+1}{k+2} \to |z-1|. Let us look at |z−1|=1|z-1|=1. Here it is interesting to point out that the series diverges at z=0z=0: f(0)=−(1+12+13+14+⋯)=−∞f(0) = -\left(1+\frac 12+\frac 13+ \frac 14+\cdots\right) = -\infty but converges conditionally for z=2z=2 f(2)=1−12+13−14+⋯=log⁡(2).f(2) = 1-\frac 12+\frac 13- \frac 14+\cdots = \log(2).

Similarly, g(z)=1+z+z2+z3+z4+⋯g(z) = 1+z+z^2+z^3+z^4+\cdots converges absolutely for |z|<1|z|<1 and diverges for |z|>1|z|>1. Additionally, the series above can be written explicitly as 11−z\frac 1{1-z} for |z|<1|z|<1.

Now there is one small peculiarity here, the power series here is defined in a disc |z|<1|z|<1 but its limit 11−z\frac1{1-z} is defined for any zz except 11. This is a general phenomenon. Power series converge absolutely inside discs, they may also converge at points on the boundary, and diverge elsewhere, so it is common for a series not to represent a function throughout the function's domain. In the case of the exponential, sine, and cosine, they converge in the whole complex plane, which we may think of as a disc of infinite radius.

The same goes for f(z)=(z−1)−(z−1)22+(z−1)33−(z−1)44+⋯,f(z) = (z-1)-\frac{(z-1)^2}{2}+\frac{(z-1)^3}{3}-\frac{(z-1)^4}{4} + \cdots, we considered above. We later will show that this series is equal to log⁡(z)\log(z) in |z−1|<1|z-1|<1 and some points on the boundary of the unit disk, but log⁡(z)\log(z) is defined in ℂ\{x+iy:x≤0 and y=0},\mathbb C \setminus \{x+iy: x\leq0 \text{ and }y=0\}, which is not obvious.
