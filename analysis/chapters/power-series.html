In this section we will discuss functions that can be written f(z)=∑k=0∞ak(z−s)k.f(z)= \sum\limits_{k=0}^\infty a_k (z-s)^k. These kinds of functions are called power series. In order to understand them, we need two important results on convergence of series. The first is about absolutely convergent series and the second is a bit less well known called the dominated convergence theorem.

Conditionally convergent series

The series is conditionally convergent when ∑k=0∞|ak|=∞and∑k=0∞akconverges.\sum\limits_{k=0}^\infty |a_k| = \infty \quad\text {and} \quad \sum\limits_{k =0 }^\infty a_k \quad\text{converges}. These conditionally convergent series are horrible to work with because rearranging their terms can change their limits.

Example

The series ∑k=0∞(−1)kk+1\sum_{k=0}^\infty \frac{(-1)^k}{k+1} is conditionally convergent. First of all, it is convergent due to the alternating series theorem and its limit is log⁡(2)\log(2), a fact we will prove later. We need to show now that the harmonic sum Hn=∑k=0n−11k+1H_n = \sum\limits_{k=0}^{n-1} \frac1{k+1} diverges.

We have Hn=11+12+13+14+⋯+1nH_n = \frac 1 1 +\frac 1 2+\frac 13+ \frac 14+\cdots +\frac 1 n Observe that 13+14>14+14=12,15+16+17+18>18+18+18+18=12,⋮∑k=2n−12n−11k+1>12for n≥2. \begin{aligned} \frac13+\frac14 &>\frac14+\frac14=\frac12,\\ \frac15+\frac16+\frac17+\frac18 &>\frac18+\frac18+\frac18+\frac18=\frac12,\\ &\vdots\\ \sum_{k=2^{n-1}}^{2^{n}-1}\frac1{k+1} &>\frac12\quad\text{for }n\geq2. \end{aligned} Therefore, ∑k=02n−11k+1≥1+n2→∞,\sum_{k=0}^{2^n-1}\frac1{k+1}\geq1+\frac n2\to\infty, and ∑k=0∞1k+1=∞.\sum_{k=0}^\infty\frac1{k+1}=\infty.

Now concerning the rearrangement:

We take the positive terms 1,13,15,…1,\frac13,\frac15,\ldots in order, placing the next two unused negative terms after each one. This gives the groups below. In each group, the first two terms combine to half of the positive term: (1−12−14)+(13−16−18)+(15−110−112)+⋯=(12−14)+(16−18)+(110−112)+⋯=12(1−12)+12(13−14)+12(15−16)+⋯=12[(1−12)+(13−14)+(15−16)+⋯]. \begin{aligned} &\left(1-\frac12-\frac14\right)+\left(\frac13-\frac16-\frac18\right)+\left(\frac15-\frac1{10}-\frac1{12}\right)+\cdots\\ &=\left(\frac12-\frac14\right)+\left(\frac16-\frac18\right)+\left(\frac1{10}-\frac1{12}\right)+\cdots\\ &=\frac12\left(1-\frac12\right)+\frac12\left(\frac13-\frac14\right)+\frac12\left(\frac15-\frac16\right)+\cdots\\ &=\frac12\left[\left(1-\frac12\right)+\left(\frac13-\frac14\right)+\left(\frac15-\frac16\right)+\cdots\right]. \end{aligned} Thus, each group of three terms becomes half of a consecutive pair in the original series. Every term is used exactly once, but after nn groups the sum is 12[(1−12)+(13−14)+⋯+(12n−1−12n)]→12log⁡(2).\frac12\left[\left(1-\frac12\right)+\left(\frac13-\frac14\right)+\cdots+\left(\frac1{2n-1}-\frac1{2n}\right)\right]\to\frac12\log(2).

In summary, rearranging the terms changed the limit from log⁡(2)\log(2) to log⁡(2)2\frac{\log(2)}2.

The study of this strange phenomenon in the series above is due to the French mathematician Augustin-Louis Cauchy.

This weird behavior due to rearranging the coefficients goes much further. The Riemann rearrangement theorem, due to the German mathematician Bernhard Riemann in the 19th century, states that we can rearrange a conditionally convergent series of real numbers to give us any real number we want, or to diverge to +∞+\infty or −∞-\infty.

Absolutely convergent series

Theorem: Consider a sequence ak∈ℂa_k \in \mathbb C. If ∑k=0∞|ak|<∞,\sum\limits_{k=0}^\infty |a_k|< \infty, meaning that this infinite sum is convergent, then the series ∑k=0∞ak\sum\limits_{k =0 }^\infty a_k converges. In this situation, we say this series is absolutely convergent. Additionally, this series does not change its limit when we rearrange its terms.

Proof:

First we show that ∑k=0∞ak\sum\limits_{k =0 }^\infty a_k converges by showing that its partial sums form a Cauchy sequence. Define Sn=∑k=0nakS_n = \sum_{k=0}^n a_k and An=∑k=0n|ak|A_n=\sum_{k=0}^n |a_k|. Consider n>mn>m. Just notice that Sn−SmS_n-S_m is the sum of the terms aia_i with m<i≤nm<i\leq n. Therefore, |Sn−Sm|≤An−Am.|S_n - S_m| \leq A_n - A_m. If m>nm>n, then |Sm−Sn|≤Am−An.|S_m-S_n| \leq A_m - A_n. In any case, |Sn−Sm|≤|An−Am|→0,|S_n-S_m| \leq |A_n-A_m| \to 0, because AnA_n converges.

Thus, SnS_n is a Cauchy sequence and consequently converges to some value SS.

Now we prove the limit of the series does not change under rearrangements. Consider a bijection σ:ℤ≥0→ℤ≥0\sigma: \mathbb Z_{\geq 0} \to \mathbb Z_{\geq 0} and define bi=aσ(i)b_i = a_{\sigma(i)}. Fix r>0r>0. There exists N∈ℤ≥0N \in \mathbb Z_{\geq 0} such that ∑k=N+1∞|ak|<rand|SN−S|<r.\sum\limits_{k=N+1}^\infty |a_k| <r \quad \text{and} \quad |S_N - S| <r. Define Tn=∑k=0nbkT_n = \sum_{k=0}^n b_k. For some N′N' sufficiently large, the list b0,…,bN′b_0,\ldots,b_{N'} contains all the terms of a0,…,aNa_0,\ldots, a_N. Therefore, for n>N′n>N' we have |Tn−S|≤|Tn−SN|+|SN−S|≤∑k=N+1∞|ak|+|SN−S|<2r.|T_n-S| \leq |T_n-S_N|+|S_N-S|\leq \sum\limits_{k=N+1}^\infty |a_k|+ |S_N-S|<2r. Therefore, Tn→ST_n \to S.

QED

We usually prove that a series is absolutely convergent using the ratio test below. The idea is to compare the tail ∑k=N∞|ak|\sum_{k=N}^\infty |a_k| of the series ∑k=0∞|ak|\sum_{k=0}^\infty |a_k|for some NN with a geometric series, with s>0s>0, ∑k=0∞sk is{∞for s>111−sfor s<1\sum_{k=0}^\infty s^k \text \quad{is}\quad \begin{cases} \infty \quad \text{for }s>1\\ \frac 1{1-s} \quad \text{for }s<1\\ \end{cases} As a matter of fact, most of the important results in series follow from comparing one unknown series with a geometric one.

Ratio test and power series

Theorem (ratio test): Assume that the ratio |ak+1||ak|\frac{|a_{k+1}|}{|a_k|} is well defined and converges to some limit LL. The series ∑k=0∞ak\sum_{k=0}^\infty a_k converges absolutely if L<1L<1 and diverges if L>1L>1.

There are other similar tests to the one above, like root test or limsup test, but I won't bother talking about them.

The exp⁡(z)\exp(z), sin⁡(θ)\sin(\theta), and cos⁡(θ)\cos(\theta) series converge for any zz or θ\theta. At zero, convergence is immediate; otherwise, we use the ratio test. For the exponential we have ak=zkk!a_k = \frac{z^k}{k!}. Thus, |ak+1||ak|=|z|k+1→0.\frac{|a_{k+1}|}{|a_k|} = \frac{|z|}{k+1} \to 0. For the sine function ak=(−1)kθ2k+1(2k+1)!a_k = \frac{(-1)^k\theta^{2k+1}}{(2k+1)!} |ak+1||ak|=|θ|2(2k+3)(2k+2)→0.\frac{|a_{k+1}|}{|a_k|} = \frac{|\theta|^2}{(2k+3)(2k+2)} \to 0. For the cosine function ak=(−1)kθ2k(2k)!a_k = \frac{(-1)^k\theta^{2k}}{(2k)!} |ak+1||ak|=|θ|2(2k+2)(2k+1)→0.\frac{|a_{k+1}|}{|a_k|} = \frac{|\theta|^2}{(2k+2)(2k+1)} \to 0. Therefore, ez=∑k=0∞zkk!,e^z = \sum_{k=0}^\infty \frac{z^k}{k!}, sin⁡(θ)=∑k=0∞(−1)kθ2k+1(2k+1)!,\sin(\theta) = \sum_{k=0}^\infty \frac{(-1)^k \theta^{2k+1}}{(2k+1)!}, cos⁡(θ)=∑k=0∞(−1)kθ2k(2k)!\cos(\theta) = \sum_{k=0}^\infty \frac{(-1)^k \theta^{2k}}{(2k)!} are absolutely convergent series. As we said before functions of the form f(z)=∑k=0∞ak(z−s)kf(z) = \sum_{k=0}^\infty a_k(z-s)^k are called power series. So, the exponential, sine, and cosine power series converge for all zz. Here I want to point out that for sin\sin and cos\cos we have been considering θ\theta real, but nothing forbids us to define these functions for complex inputs via the power series. So, sin\sin and cos\cos are in fact complex valued functions as well.

Another example is f(z)=∑k=0∞(−1)k(z−1)k+1k+1,f(z) = \sum_{k=0}^\infty\frac{(-1)^k(z-1)^{k+1}}{k+1}, this function converges absolutely for |z−1|<1|z-1|<1 and diverges for |z−1|>1|z-1|>1. The case z=1z=1 is immediate. For z≠1z\neq1, |z−1|k+2/(k+2)|z−1|k+1/(k+1)=|z−1|k+1k+2→|z−1|.\frac{|z-1|^{k+2}/(k+2)}{|z-1|^{k+1}/(k+1)} = |z-1| \frac{k+1}{k+2} \to |z-1|. Let us look at |z−1|=1|z-1|=1. Here it is interesting to point out that the series diverges at z=0z=0: f(0)=−∑k=0∞1k+1=−∞f(0) = -\sum_{k=0}^\infty \frac{1}{k+1} = -\infty but converges conditionally for z=2z=2 f(2)=∑k=0∞(−1)kk+1.f(2) = \sum_{k=0}^\infty \frac{(-1)^k}{k+1}.

Similarly, f(z)=∑k=0∞zkf(z) = \sum_{k=0}^\infty z^k converges absolutely for |z|<1|z|<1 and diverges for |z|>1|z|>1. Additionally, the series above can be written explicitly as 11−z\frac 1{1-z} for |z|<1|z|<1.

Now there is one small peculiarity here, the power series here is defined in a disc |z|<1|z|<1 but its limit 11−z\frac1{1-z} is defined for any zz except 11. This is a general phenomenon. Power series converge absolutely inside discs, they may also converge at points on the boundary, and diverge elsewhere, so it is common for a series not to represent a function throughout the function's domain. In the case of the exponential, sine, and cosine, they converge in the whole complex plane, which we may think of as a disc of infinite radius.

The same goes to f(z)=∑k=0∞(−1)k(z−1)k+1k+1.f(z) = \sum_{k=0}^\infty\frac{(-1)^k(z-1)^{k+1}}{k+1}. We later will show that this series is equal to log⁡(z)\log(z) for |z−1|<1|z-1|<1, but log⁡(z)\log(z) is defined in ℂ\{x+iy:x≤0 and y=0},\mathbb C \setminus \{x+iy: x\leq0 \text{ and }y=0\}, which is not obvious.
