Exponentials as limits

Now we discuss exponentials y=axy=a^x, where a>0a>0.

All these curves pass through (0,1)(0,1) because a0=1a^0=1. For a>1a>1 the function is increasing, and for 0<a<10<a<1 it is decreasing. For a=1a=1 it is constant.

At (0,1)(0,1) we can draw a tangent line y=1+kxy=1+kx. For a>1a>1, the slope kk is positive, and for 0<a<10<a<1, the slope kk is negative. In both cases, the curve is on or above the line: ax≥1+kx.a^x\geq 1+kx.

When uu is very small, au≃1+kua^u\simeq 1+ku. For any fixed xx and very large nn, u=xnu = \frac x n is close to 00 and ax/n≃1+kxn,ax≃(1+kxn)n.a^{x/n}\simeq 1+\frac{kx}{n}, \qquad a^x\simeq\left(1+\frac{kx}{n}\right)^n. This leads to the following conjecture: (1+kxn)n→ax.\left(1+\frac{kx}{n}\right)^n\to a^x.

Theorem: For every real xx, (1+kxn)n→ax.\left(1+\frac{kx}{n}\right)^n\to a^x.

Proof:

Assume a>1a>1. The slope kk must be positive. We can also take nn large enough that 1+kxn>01+\frac{kx}{n}>0.

Since ax/n≥1+kxn,a^{x/n}\geq 1+\frac{kx}{n}, we get (1+kxn)n≤ax.\left(1+\frac{kx}{n}\right)^n\leq a^x.

Now we need a lower bound that tends to axa^x. ay/n≤1+ky/n1−ky/n,a^{y/n}\leq1+\frac{ky/n}{1-ky/n},

For 0<a<10<a<1, write b=1/a>1b=1/a>1.

Reflecting the graph y=axy=a^x across the yy-axis, by (x,y)↦(−x,y)(x,y)\mapsto(-x,y), gives y=a−xy=a^{-x}. The same reflection sends the tangent line y=1+kxy=1+kx to y=1−kxy=1-kx. So the result holds for every a>0a>0. QED

Exponentials as series

Now we provide our first important result about series.

Theorem: The exponential exe^x can be written as ex=∑k=0∞xkk!.e^x = \sum\limits_{k=0}^\infty \frac{x^k}{k!}.

Proof:

Write Sn(x)=1+x+x22!+⋯+xnn!.S_n(x)=1+x+\frac{x^2}{2!}+\cdots+\frac{x^n}{n!}. Suppose first that x≥0x\geq0. We will compare this sum with two binomial expansions: (1+xn)n≤Sn(x)≤(1+xn2)n2+n.\left(1+\frac xn\right)^n \leq S_n(x) \leq\left(1+\frac{x}{n^2}\right)^{n^2+n}.

For the first inequality, the coefficient of xkx^k in (1+x/n)n(1+x/n)^n is (nk)nk=1k!n(n−1)⋯(n−k+1)nk≤1k!,1≤k≤n,\begin{align} \frac{\binom nk}{n^k} &=\frac1{k!}\frac{n(n-1)\cdots(n-k+1)}{n^k}\\ &\leq\frac1{k!},\qquad 1\leq k\leq n, \end{align} because each of the kk numerator factors is at most nn. The constant terms are both 11. Multiplying these coefficient inequalities by xk≥0x^k\geq0 and adding gives the first inequality.

For the second inequality, the coefficient of xkx^k in (1+x/n2)n2+n(1+x/n^2)^{n^2+n} is (n2+nk)(n2)k=1k!(n2+n)(n2+n−1)⋯(n2+n−k+1)(n2)k.\frac{\binom{n^2+n}{k}}{(n^2)^k} =\frac1{k!}\frac{(n^2+n)(n^2+n-1)\cdots(n^2+n-k+1)}{(n^2)^k}. When 1≤k≤n1\leq k\leq n, even the smallest numerator factor satisfies n2+n−k+1≥n2+1>n2.n^2+n-k+1\geq n^2+1>n^2. Thus this coefficient is at least 1/k!1/k!. The terms through degree nn therefore add up to at least Sn(x)S_n(x), and all the higher-degree terms are nonnegative. This proves the second inequality.

The lower bound tends to exe^x. To find the limit of the upper bound, write (1+xn2)n2+n=(1+xn2)n2(1+xn2)n.\left(1+\frac{x}{n^2}\right)^{n^2+n} =\left(1+\frac{x}{n^2}\right)^{n^2} \left(1+\frac{x}{n^2}\right)^n. The first factor tends to exe^x, by the known exponential limit along the indices n2n^2. For the second factor, observe that 11+x/n2=n2n2+x=1−xn2+x.\begin{align} \frac1{1+x/n^2}&=\frac{n^2}{n^2+x}\\ &=1-\frac{x}{n^2+x}. \end{align} Since 0≤x/(n2+x)<10\leq x/(n^2+x)<1, Bernoulli's inequality gives 1−nxn2+x≤(1−xn2+x)n=1(1+x/n2)n≤1.\begin{align} 1-\frac{nx}{n^2+x} &\leq\left(1-\frac{x}{n^2+x}\right)^n\\ &=\frac1{(1+x/n^2)^n}\leq1. \end{align} Here nx/(n2+x)→0nx/(n^2+x)\to0, so the reciprocal tends to 11, and hence (1+x/n2)n→1(1+x/n^2)^n\to1. Both bounds on Sn(x)S_n(x) therefore tend to exe^x, proving Sn(x)→exS_n(x)\to e^x for x≥0x\geq0.

Now suppose x<0x<0. Subtracting the binomial expansion from the partial sum gives Sn(x)−(1+xn)n=∑k=0n(1k!−(nk)nk)xk.S_n(x)-\left(1+\frac xn\right)^n =\sum_{k=0}^n\left(\frac1{k!}-\frac{\binom nk}{n^k}\right)x^k. Every coefficient in parentheses is nonnegative, as shown above. Thus taking absolute values term by term replaces xkx^k by |x|k|x|^k, without changing these coefficients. By the triangle inequality, |Sn(x)−(1+xn)n|≤∑k=0n(1k!−(nk)nk)|x|k=Sn(|x|)−(1+|x|n)n→0.\begin{align} \left|S_n(x)-\left(1+\frac xn\right)^n\right| &\leq\sum_{k=0}^n\left(\frac1{k!}-\frac{\binom nk}{n^k}\right)|x|^k\\ &=S_n(|x|)-\left(1+\frac{|x|}{n}\right)^n\to0. \end{align} The last difference tends to 00 because both terms tend to e|x|e^{|x|}, by the case already proved and the exponential limit. Finally, (1+x/n)n→ex(1+x/n)^n\to e^x, so Sn(x)→exS_n(x)\to e^x for negative xx as well.

QED
