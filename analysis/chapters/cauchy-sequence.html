Double sequences

Consider a double sequence pnmp_{nm} with n,m≥1n,m \geq 1.

p11p12p13⋯p21p22p23⋯p31p32p33⋯⋮⋮⋮⋱ \begin{array}{cccc} p_{11} & p_{12} & p_{13} & \cdots \\ p_{21} & p_{22} & p_{23} & \cdots \\ p_{31} & p_{32} & p_{33} & \cdots \\ \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \ddots \end{array} We say that pnm→pp_{nm} \to p if the following statement is true: r>0⇒p−r<pnm<p+rfor n,m sufficiently large. r>0 \quad \Longrightarrow \quad p-r<p_{nm}<p+r \quad \text{for } n,m\text{ sufficiently large}. More precisely, for every r>0r>0, there exists NN such that p−r<pnm<p+rp-r<p_{nm}<p+r for n,m≥Nn,m\geq N.

For instance, pnm=1m+n→0p_{nm}=\frac1{m+n}\to0. On the other hand, pnm=nn+mp_{nm}=\frac n{n+m} does not converge. Just compare pnn=12p_{nn}=\frac12 with pn2n=n2n2+n→1p_{n^2\,n}=\frac{n^2}{n^2+n}\to1. Also, pnm=mnm+n=11n+1m→∞p_{nm}=\frac{mn}{m+n}=\frac{1}{\frac1n+\frac1m}\to\infty.

Theorem: If a sequence xnx_n converges, then xn−xm→0x_n-x_m \to 0.

Proof:

Consider r>0r>0. We have x−r2<xn<x+r2for n sufficiently large.x-\frac{r}2<x_n<x+\frac{r}2 \quad \text{for }n\text{ sufficiently large}. Therefore, xn−xm<x+r2−(x−r2)=r,x_n-x_m < x+\frac r2 - \left(x-\frac r2\right) = r, xn−xm>x−r2−(x+r2)=−rx_n-x_m > x-\frac r2 - \left(x+\frac r2\right) = -r for n,mn,m sufficiently large. QED

Cauchy sequences and completeness

Definition: A sequence xnx_n is a Cauchy sequence if xn−xm→0x_n - x_m \to 0.

Completeness hypothesis: For real numbers, every Cauchy sequence converges.

If we construct the real numbers from the rational numbers, this property can be proved. Since we are not building the real numbers from scratch here, we will have to accept this as a fact.

Here is how this is used. Imagine we don't know what π\pi is. We can construct this number as the limit of a Cauchy sequence. In this case, we actually constructed two Cauchy sequences, AnA_n and BnB_n, from the areas of polygons approximating the circle. In summary, by proving that AnA_n and BnB_n are Cauchy sequences, we could have defined π\pi as their limit. In the same way, we could have defined exe^x as the limit of qn=(1+xn)nq_n=\left(1+\frac xn\right)^n by proving that qnq_n is a Cauchy sequence and, as a consequence, convergent.

Let me give an example. Consider xn+1=1+xnx_{n+1} = \sqrt{1+x_n} with x1=ax_1=a for some positive aa. Since x2−x−1=0x^2-x-1 =0 has roots x=−1±52x=\frac{-1\pm\sqrt 5}2, we have x2−x−1>0x^2-x-1>0 for x≥1x\geq 1, meaning x>1+xx> \sqrt{1+x} for x>1x>1. Now, for n>1n>1 we have xn=1+xn−1>1x_n = \sqrt{1+x_{n-1}} >1. Therefore, xn+1=1+xn>xnx_n+1 = \sqrt{1+x_n} >x_n for n>1n>1.

Using xn≥xnx_n \geq \sqrt {x_n} and xn+1+xn≥2\sqrt{x_n+1}+\sqrt{x_n}\geq2 for n>2n>2, we have xn+1−xn=1+xn−xn=(1+xn−xn)(1+xn+xn)1+xn+xn=11+xn+xn≤12.x_{n+1} - x_n = \sqrt{1+x_n}-\sqrt{x_n} = \frac{(\sqrt{1+x_n}-\sqrt{x_n})(\sqrt{1+x_n}+\sqrt{x_n})}{\sqrt{1+x_n}+\sqrt{x_n}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1+x_n}+\sqrt{x_n}} \leq \frac 12.

Consider the polynomial p(x)=x3−3x+1p(x)=x^3-3x+1. We want to construct a root for this polynomial. Since p(0)=1p(0)=1 and p(1)=−1p(1)=-1, we expect the graph to cross zero somewhere between 00 and 11. The question is where.

We start with [0,1][0,1] and cut it in half. Which half should we keep? The one where the values of pp at the two endpoints have opposite signs. This is what guides the whole construction. We keep narrowing the interval, always with pp positive at the left endpoint and negative at the right endpoint.

Let us write the rule explicitly. Suppose our current interval is [r,s][r,s], with p(r)>0,p(s)<0.p(r)>0,\qquad p(s)<0.

  1. Take its middle point, xn=r+s2x_n=\frac{r+s}{2}, and calculate p(xn)p(x_n).
  2. If p(xn)=0p(x_n)=0, we have found a root and stop.
  3. If p(xn)<0p(x_n)<0, keep [r,xn][r,x_n]: p(r)>0p(r)>0 and p(xn)<0p(x_n)<0. The new right endpoint is xnx_n.

But is aa a root? Call the endpoints of the interval after nn steps rnr_n and sns_n. They approach the same aa, since their distance from xnx_n is at most 1/2n1/2^n. Sequences over complex numbers

A sequence of complex numbers zn=an+ibnz_n = a_n+ib_n converges to z=a+ibz=a+ib if an→aa_n \to a and bn→bb_n \to b. We denote this by zn→zz_n \to z. Equivalently, zn→zz_n \to z if |z−zn|=(an−a)2+(bn−b)2→0.|z-z_n| = \sqrt{(a_n-a)^2+(b_n-b)^2} \to 0. On the other hand, znz_n diverges to infinity, zn→∞z_n \to \infty, if |zn|→∞|z_n| \to \infty.

For instance, n+inn2+ni→0\frac {n+i\sqrt n} {n^2+n i} \to 0. We can see this in two ways. Brute force: n+inn2+ni=(n+in)(n2−in)n4+n2=n3+n3/2n4+n2+in5/2−n2n4+n2=1n+1n5/21+1n2+i1n3/2−1n21+1n2→0.\begin{align} \frac{n+i\sqrt n}{n^2+ni} &=\frac{(n+i\sqrt n)(n^2-in)}{n^4+n^2}\\ &=\frac{n^3+n^{3/2}}{n^4+n^2}+i\frac{n^{5/2}-n^2}{n^4+n^2}\\ &=\frac{\frac1n+\frac1{n^{5/2}}}{1+\frac1{n^2}}+i\frac{\frac1{n^{3/2}}-\frac1{n^2}}{1+\frac1{n^2}}\to0. \end{align} Using the norm: |n+inn2+ni|=n2+nn4+n2=1n1+1n1+1n2→0.\begin{align} \left|\frac{n+i\sqrt n}{n^2+ni}\right| &=\frac{\sqrt{n^2+n}}{\sqrt{n^4+n^2}}\\ &=\frac1n\frac{\sqrt{1+\frac1n}}{\sqrt{1+\frac1{n^2}}}\to0. \end{align}

The rules for limits of sums, products, and conjugates follow from the corresponding rules for real sequences. Indeed, addition and multiplication express the real and imaginary parts as sums and products of real sequences, while conjugation changes an+ibna_n+ib_n to an−ibna_n-ib_n.

Similarly, znz_n is a Cauchy sequence if |zn−zm|→0|z_n-z_m| \to 0 as n,m→∞n,m\to\infty, which is the same as saying that ana_n and bnb_n are Cauchy sequences. Since the real numbers are complete, both ana_n and bnb_n converge. Hence znz_n converges, so the complex numbers are also complete.
