Sequences over complex numbers

A sequence of complex numbers zn=xn+iynz_n = x_n+iy_n converges to z=x+iyz=x+iy if xn→xx_n \to x and yn→yy_n \to y. We denote this by zn→zz_n \to z. Equivalently, zn→zz_n \to z if |z−zn|=(xn−x)2+(yn−y)2→0.|z-z_n| = \sqrt{(x_n-x)^2+(y_n-y)^2} \to 0. On the other hand, we say znz_n diverges to infinity, zn→∞z_n \to \infty, when |zn|→∞|z_n| \to \infty.

The rules for limits of sums, products, and conjugates follow from the corresponding rules for real sequences. Indeed, addition and multiplication express the real and imaginary parts as sums and products of real sequences, while conjugation changes xn+iynx_n+iy_n to xn−iynx_n-iy_n.

Similarly, znz_n is a Cauchy sequence if |zn−zm|→0|z_n-z_m| \to 0 as n,m→∞n,m\to\infty, which is the same as saying that xnx_n and yny_n are Cauchy sequences. Since the real numbers are complete, both xnx_n and yny_n converge. Hence znz_n converges, so the complex numbers are also complete.

Geometrically, the complex number z=x+iyz = x+iy can be represented in the plane and written in polar coordinates when z≠0z \neq 0. Indeed, by writing x=rcos⁡(α),y=rsin⁡(α),r=x2+y2x=r\cos(\alpha), \quad y = r\sin(\alpha), \quad r= \sqrt{x^2+y^2} we get z=r(cos⁡(α)+isin⁡(α)).z=r(\cos(\alpha)+i\sin(\alpha)).

Using cos⁡(α+β)=cos⁡(α)cos⁡(β)−sin⁡(α)sin⁡(β),\cos(\alpha+\beta) = \cos(\alpha)\cos(\beta) - \sin(\alpha)\sin(\beta), sin⁡(α+β)=cos⁡(α)sin⁡(β)+sin⁡(α)cos⁡(β)\sin(\alpha+\beta) = \cos(\alpha)\sin(\beta) + \sin(\alpha)\cos(\beta) we obtain (cos⁡(α)+isin⁡(α))(cos⁡(β)+isin⁡(β))=cos⁡(α+β)+isin⁡(α+β).(\cos(\alpha)+i \sin(\alpha))(\cos(\beta)+i \sin(\beta)) = \cos(\alpha+\beta)+i \sin(\alpha+\beta). For two non-zero complex numbers z=r(cos⁡(α)+isin⁡(α)),w=s(cos⁡(β)+isin⁡(β))z=r(\cos(\alpha)+i\sin(\alpha)), \quad w=s(\cos(\beta)+i\sin(\beta)) their product is zw=rs(cos⁡(α+β)+isin⁡(α+β)).zw = rs(\cos(\alpha+\beta) + i \sin(\alpha+\beta)).

So, to multiply two complex numbers, we multiply their radii and add their angles.

Example

Consider c=r(cos⁡(θ)+isin⁡(θ))c= r(\cos(\theta)+i \sin(\theta)).

Define zn=1+c+c2+⋯+cn−1nz_n = \frac{1+c+c^2+\cdots+c^{n-1}}n.

The number znz_n is the average of the points 1,c,c2,…,cn−11,c,c^2,\ldots,c^{n-1}. For c=1c=1, it is clear that znz_n is constant and equal to 11. So, we can assume c≠1c\neq 1.

Since 1+c+c2+⋯+cn−1=cn−1c−1,1+c+c^2+\cdots+c^{n-1} = \frac{c^n-1}{c-1}, we get zn=cn−1n(c−1).z_n=\frac{c^n-1}{n(c-1)}. If r≤1r\leq1, then |zn|=|cn−1|n|c−1|≤rn+1n|c−1|≤2n|c−1|→0.|z_n|=\frac{|c^n-1|}{n|c-1|}\leq\frac{r^n+1}{n|c-1|}\leq\frac2{n|c-1|}\to0. Thus, zn→0z_n\to0.

If r>1r>1, the reverse triangle inequality gives |zn|≥rn−1n|c−1|.|z_n|\geq\frac{r^n-1}{n|c-1|}. By the binomial theorem, for n≥2n\geq2, rn=(1+(r−1))n≥1+n(r−1)+n(n−1)2(r−1)2.r^n=(1+(r-1))^n\geq1+n(r-1)+\frac{n(n-1)}2(r-1)^2. Therefore, |zn|≥(r−1)+n−12(r−1)2|c−1|→∞.|z_n|\geq\frac{(r-1)+\frac{n-1}2(r-1)^2}{|c-1|}\to\infty. In conclusion, zn→{1,c=1,0,|c|≤1,c≠1,∞,|c|>1.z_n\longrightarrow \begin{cases} 1,&c=1,\\ 0,&|c|\leq1,\ c\neq1,\\ \infty,&|c|>1. \end{cases}

Complex exponential

Now, we define the exponential of complex numbers.

Theorem 5.1: The sequence

qn=(1+zn)nq_n = \left(1+\frac zn\right)^n is a Cauchy sequence. Its limit is denoted by eze^z.

Proof:

By the binomial theorem, (1+zn)n=1+z+(n2)z2n2+(n3)z3n3+⋯+(nn)znnn=1+z+a2z2+a3z3+⋯+anzn,\begin{align}\left(1+\frac zn\right)^n &= 1+z+\binom{n}{2}\frac{z^2}{n^2}+\binom{n}{3}\frac{z^3}{n^3}+\cdots+\binom{n}{n}\frac{z^n}{n^n} \\&=1+z+a_2z^2+a_3z^3+\cdots+a_nz^n,\end{align} where ak=(nk)nk=1k!n(n−1)⋯(n−k+1)nk=1k!(1−1n)(1−2n)⋯(1−k−1n).\begin{align}a_k = \frac{\binom nk}{n^k} &= \frac 1{k!}\frac{n(n-1)\cdots(n-k+1)}{n^k} \\&= \frac 1{k!}\left(1-\frac 1n\right)\left(1-\frac 2n\right)\cdots\left(1-\frac {k-1}n\right).\end{align}

Take m>nm>n. For 2≤k≤n2\leq k\leq n, each factor in the coefficient of zkz^k satisfies 0≤1−jn≤1−jm(1≤j≤k−1).0\leq1-\frac jn\leq1-\frac jm \qquad (1\leq j\leq k-1). Multiplying these inequalities shows that the coefficient of zkz^k in (1+zm)m\left(1+\frac zm\right)^m is at least as large as its coefficient in (1+zn)n\left(1+\frac zn\right)^n. The terms of degrees n+1,…,mn+1,\ldots,m occur only in the first polynomial and also have positive coefficients. The constant and linear terms cancel.

Thus, we can write qm−qn=b2z2+⋯+bmzm,q_m-q_n=b_2z^2+\cdots+b_mz^m, where every bk≥0b_k\geq0. By the triangle inequality, |qm−qn|≤b2|z|2+⋯+bm|z|m.|q_m-q_n|\leq b_2|z|^2+\cdots+b_m|z|^m. The sum on the right is exactly the same polynomial difference evaluated at |z||z| instead of zz. Therefore, |qm−qn|≤(1+|z|m)m−(1+|z|n)n.|q_m-q_n| \leq\left(1+\frac{|z|}{m}\right)^m -\left(1+\frac{|z|}{n}\right)^n. We already know that the real sequence (1+|z|n)n\left(1+\frac{|z|}{n}\right)^n converges to e|z|e^{|z|}. Consequently, the right-hand side tends to zero as n,m→∞n,m\to\infty. The case n>mn>m follows by interchanging the indices. Hence qnq_n is a Cauchy sequence. QED

Note that ez¯=ez¯\overline{e^z} = e^{\overline z}. Indeed, (1+zn)n¯=(1+z¯n)n,\overline{\left(1+\frac zn\right)^n} =\left(1+\frac{\overline z}{n}\right)^n, and taking the limit gives the identity.

Euler Formula

To understand the complex exponential, it remains to find eiθe^{i\theta} for real θ\theta. We will show that eiθ=cos⁡(θ)+isin⁡(θ).e^{i \theta} = \cos(\theta) + i \sin(\theta). This is Euler's formula: eiθe^{i\theta} is the point on the unit circle obtained by rotating 11 through θ\theta radians.

Theorem 5.3: eiθ=cos⁡(θ)+isin⁡(θ)e^{i\theta} = \cos(\theta) + i \sin(\theta)

Proof:

Let us define f(θ)=cos⁡(θ)+isin⁡(θ)f(\theta) = \cos(\theta)+i \sin(\theta).

For 0<θ<π/20<\theta<\pi/2, the blue region below is the difference between the sector and the triangle. Its area is (θ−sin⁡θ)/2(\theta-\sin\theta)/2. It lies inside the rectangle of width 1−cos⁡θ1-\cos\theta and height sin⁡θ\sin\theta.

Comparing areas, 0≤θ−sin⁡θ2≤(1−cos⁡θ)sin⁡θ≤1−cos⁡θ.0\leq\frac{\theta-\sin\theta}{2}\leq(1-\cos\theta)\sin\theta\leq1-\cos\theta. We already know that |sin⁡x|≤|x||\sin x|\leq|x|. Hence 0≤1−cos⁡θ=2sin⁡2(θ/2)≤θ22,0\leq1-\cos\theta=2\sin^2(\theta/2)\leq\frac{\theta^2}{2}, and consequently 0≤θ−sin⁡θ≤θ2.0\leq\theta-\sin\theta\leq\theta^2. For −π/2<θ<0-\pi/2<\theta<0, apply these bounds to −θ-\theta. Since sin⁡(−θ)=−sin⁡θ\sin(-\theta)=-\sin\theta and cos⁡(−θ)=cos⁡θ\cos(-\theta)=\cos\theta, |sin⁡θ−θ|=|sin⁡(−θ)−(−θ)|≤θ2,|1−cos⁡θ|=|1−cos⁡(−θ)|≤θ22.\begin{align} |\sin\theta-\theta|&=|\sin(-\theta)-(-\theta)|\leq\theta^2,\\ |1-\cos\theta|&=|1-\cos(-\theta)|\leq\frac{\theta^2}{2}. \end{align} Both differences are zero at θ=0\theta=0. Thus, for |θ|<π/2|\theta|<\pi/2, |f(θ)−(1+iθ)|≤|cos⁡θ−1|+|sin⁡θ−θ|≤32θ2.\begin{align} |f(\theta)-(1+i\theta)| &\leq|\cos\theta-1|+|\sin\theta-\theta|\\ &\leq\frac32\theta^2. \end{align}

First observe that f(α+β)=f(α)f(β).f(\alpha+\beta) = f(\alpha)f(\beta).

Additionally, f(0)=1f(0) = 1 and |f(θ)|=1|f(\theta)| = 1.

Now fix a real θ\theta. Since f(θ)=f(θ/n)nf(\theta)=f(\theta/n)^n, we compare f(θ/n)nf(\theta/n)^n with (1+iθ/n)n(1+i\theta/n)^n using an−bn=(a−b)(an−1+an−2b+⋯+bn−1).a^n-b^n=(a-b)(a^{n-1}+a^{n-2}b+\cdots+b^{n-1}). Take a=f(θ/n)a=f(\theta/n) and b=1+iθ/nb=1+i\theta/n. Since |a|=1|a|=1 and |b|≤1+|θ|/n|b|\leq1+|\theta|/n, each of the nn terms in parentheses has absolute value at most (1+|θ|/n)n−1(1+|\theta|/n)^{n-1}. Therefore, eiθ=f(θ)=cos⁡θ+isin⁡θ.e^{i\theta}=f(\theta)=\cos\theta+i\sin\theta. QED
