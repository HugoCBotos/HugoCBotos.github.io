2. What is Bayesian Inference?

🎯 In one sentence

Bayesian inference is a framework for updating your beliefs about something (a weight, a parameter) when you see new data. You start with a prior belief, see data, and end with a posterior belief.

The Three Ingredients

📝 Notation: the vertical bar "|" means "given"

In probability, p(D | w) reads as "probability of D given w". The pipe | separates what we're computing (left) from what we assume known (right). So p(D | w) = "how likely is the data D, assuming the weights are w".

Every Bayesian problem has three components:

NameSymbolMeaningAnalogy
Prior p(w) What we believe about w before seeing data "Before the experiment, I think the coin is probably fair"
Likelihood p(D|w) How probable is the data D if w were the true value "If the coin had bias 0.7, what's the chance of 8 heads in 10 tosses?"
Posterior p(w|D) What we believe after seeing the data "After seeing 8 heads in 10 tosses, now I think the bias is around 0.8"

Bayes' Theorem

The three ingredients are connected by Bayes' theorem:

Bayes' Theorem
$$p(w \mid D) = \frac{p(D \mid w) \, p(w)}{p(D)}$$

In words:

Posterior = (Likelihood × Prior) / Evidence
  • Posterior $p(w \mid D)$: our updated belief after seeing data
  • Likelihood $p(D \mid w)$: how well the data fits a particular $w$
  • Prior $p(w)$: our belief before data
  • Evidence $p(D)$: a normalizing constant (total probability of the data)
🔍 For the mathematician: The evidence $p(D) = \int p(D \mid w) \, p(w) \, dw$ is just the constant that makes the posterior integrate to 1. It's often impossible to compute analytically — that's the central difficulty of Bayesian inference.

A Concrete Example: Rolling a Die

Scenario: A six-sided die is rolled 60 times. The observed counts are: 15 ones, 10 twos, 8 threes, 12 fours, 7 fives, 8 sixes. What is the probability vector p = (p₁, p₂, p₃, p₄, p₅, p₆)?

Frequentist answer (MLE): Each probability is estimated by the observed frequency:

$$\mathbf{\hat{p}} = \left(\frac{15}{60}, \frac{10}{60}, \frac{8}{60}, \frac{12}{60}, \frac{7}{60}, \frac{8}{60}\right) = (0.25, 0.17, 0.13, 0.20, 0.12, 0.13)$$

A single point estimate. No notion of uncertainty — unless one computes confidence intervals from the multinomial distribution (which requires asymptotic approximations).

Bayesian answer: A whole distribution over all possible dice.

  1. Prior: Before rolling, any probability vector is equally plausible. This is modeled by a Dir(1,1,1,1,1,1) distribution — the uniform distribution over the 6-simplex (the set of all possible dice).
  2. Likelihood: Given a specific probability vector $\mathbf{p}$, the probability of the observed counts is given by the multinomial distribution: $p(D \mid \mathbf{p}) \propto p_1^{15} \cdot p_2^{10} \cdot p_3^{8} \cdot p_4^{12} \cdot p_5^{7} \cdot p_6^{8}$.
  3. Posterior: Multiply and normalize. The result is a Dir(16, 11, 9, 13, 8, 9) distribution — a continuous distribution over the simplex that concentrates near the observed frequencies but retains a spread reflecting remaining uncertainty.

Why the die is a better example than a coin:

  • With a coin, the parameter is a single number $w \in [0, 1]$ — the frequentist can give a full confidence interval from the Binomial distribution, so the Bayesian advantage seems small.
  • With a die, the parameter is a 6-dimensional vector $\mathbf{p} \in \Delta^5$ (the 5-simplex). The frequentist gives only a point estimate. The Bayesian gives a full posterior over the simplex — a much sharper contrast.
  • From the Bayesian posterior, one can answer questions like: "what is the probability that the die is biased toward even numbers? (i.e., that $p_2 + p_4 + p_6 > 0.5$)?" — a question the MLE cannot directly address.

Notice: the Bayesian answer is a distribution, not a point. That distribution tells us not just "the most likely probability vector" but also how much uncertainty remains about each face. This uncertainty information is what BNNs bring to neural networks — but instead of 6 categories (die faces), they deal with millions of parameters (weights).

Connection to Neural Networks

In a neural network, $w$ is the set of all weights (millions of numbers). The data $D$ is the training dataset.

  • Prior $p(w)$: our initial belief about the weights (usually "they should be small", i.e., a Gaussian centered at 0)
  • Likelihood $p(D \mid w)$: how well the network predicts the training data given those weights
  • Posterior $p(w \mid D)$: the distribution of weights that explain the data

A Bayesian neural network doesn't just find the single best $w$. It approximates the whole posterior $p(w \mid D)$. When making a prediction, we average over all plausible weights:

Predictive Distribution
$$p(y^* \mid x^*, D) = \int p(y^* \mid x^*, w) \, p(w \mid D) \, dw$$

This integral is why we need approximation methods (VI, Dropout, Laplace, HMC). Each method is a different way of approximating this impossible integral.

⚠️ The central challenge: The posterior p(w|D) for a neural network is a distribution over millions of parameters, with complex correlations and multiple modes. It is intractable — we can't compute it exactly. All BNN methods are approximations.
