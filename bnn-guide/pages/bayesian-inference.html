2. What is Bayesian Inference?

🎯 In one sentence

Bayesian inference is a framework for updating your beliefs about something (a weight, a parameter) when you see new data. You start with a prior belief, see data, and end with a posterior belief.

The Three Ingredients

📝 Notation: the vertical bar "|" means "given"

In probability, p(D | w) reads as "probability of D given w". The pipe | separates what we're computing (left) from what we assume known (right). So p(D | w) = "how likely is the data D, assuming the weights are w".

Every Bayesian problem has three components:

NameSymbolMeaningAnalogy
Prior p(w) What we believe about w before seeing data "Before the experiment, I think the coin is probably fair"
Likelihood p(D|w) How probable is the data D if w were the true value "If the coin had bias 0.7, what's the chance of 8 heads in 10 tosses?"
Posterior p(w|D) What we believe after seeing the data "After seeing 8 heads in 10 tosses, now I think the bias is around 0.8"

Bayes' Theorem

The three ingredients are connected by Bayes' theorem:

Bayes' Theorem
p(w | D) = p(D | w) · p(w) / p(D)

In words:

Posterior = (Likelihood × Prior) / Evidence
  • Posterior p(w|D): our updated belief after seeing data
  • Likelihood p(D|w): how well the data fits a particular w
  • Prior p(w): our belief before data
  • Evidence p(D): a normalizing constant (total probability of the data)
🔍 For the mathematician: The evidence p(D) = ∫ p(D|w) p(w) dw is just the constant that makes the posterior integrate to 1. It's often impossible to compute analytically — that's the central difficulty of Bayesian inference.

A Concrete Example: Coin Tossing

Scenario: You have a coin. You want to know its probability of landing heads (call it w). You toss it 10 times and get 8 heads. What is w?

Frequentist answer: w = 8/10 = 0.8. A single number.

Bayesian answer: We keep a whole distribution.

  1. Prior: Before tossing, we think any w ∈ [0, 1] is equally likely. So p(w) = 1 (uniform distribution).
  2. Likelihood: The probability of exactly 8 heads in 10 tosses, given bias w, is p(D|w) = C(10,8) w8 (1-w)2.
  3. Posterior: Multiply and normalize. The result is a Beta distribution concentrated near 0.8, but with a spread that reflects our remaining uncertainty.

Notice: the Bayesian answer is a distribution, not a point. That distribution tells us not just "the most likely value is 0.8" but also "we're about 95% sure the true bias is between 0.55 and 0.95". This uncertainty information is what BNNs bring to neural networks.

Connection to Neural Networks

In a neural network, w is the set of all weights (millions of numbers). The data D is the training dataset.

  • Prior p(w): our initial belief about the weights (usually "they should be small", i.e., a Gaussian centered at 0)
  • Likelihood p(D|w): how well the network predicts the training data given those weights
  • Posterior p(w|D): the distribution of weights that explain the data

A Bayesian neural network doesn't just find the single best w. It approximates the whole posterior p(w|D). When making a prediction, we average over all plausible weights:

Predictive Distribution
p(y*|x*, D) = ∫ p(y*|x*, w) p(w|D) dw

This integral is why we need approximation methods (VI, Dropout, Laplace, HMC). Each method is a different way of approximating this impossible integral.

⚠️ The central challenge: The posterior p(w|D) for a neural network is a distribution over millions of parameters, with complex correlations and multiple modes. It is intractable — we can't compute it exactly. All BNN methods are approximations.
