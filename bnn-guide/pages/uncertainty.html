4. Types of Uncertainty

🎯 In one sentence

There are two kinds of uncertainty: aleatoric (irreducible, due to noise in the data) and epistemic (reducible, due to lack of knowledge). BNNs capture epistemic uncertainty; normal NNs only capture aleatoric.

📝 Notation note: what does "y | x, w" mean?

Throughout this page you'll see expressions like Var[ y | x, w ]. The vertical bar | means "given" or "conditional on".

  • y | x, w = "the value of y, given that we know x and w"
  • Var[ y | x, w ] = "the variance of y, assuming x and w are fixed"
  • Varw ~ p(w|D)[ y | x, w ] = "the variance of y (knowing x and w), but now w itself is random — so we average over w"

The pipe | is the same notation used in conditional probability: p(y|x) means "probability of y given x". Everything to the right of | is assumed known.

Aleatoric Uncertainty (Data Noise)

Analogy: Imagine measuring the height of a random person. Even if you have the perfect measuring tool, people have different heights. There's inherent randomness in the process. That's aleatoric uncertainty.

Characteristics:

  • Irreducible — you can't eliminate it by collecting more data
  • Comes from inherent noise in the data-generating process
  • Examples: sensor noise, natural variation in measurements
  • In neural networks: the uncertainty of the output given fixed weights
Aleatoric uncertainty — noise in the data
$$\operatorname{Var}[y \mid x, w]$$
(= variance of the prediction $y$, given that we know the input $x$ and the weights $w$; this is the inherent noise that remains even with perfect weights)

A normal neural network can model aleatoric uncertainty by predicting a distribution (e.g., mean and variance) instead of just a point. This is called heteroscedastic regression.

Epistemic Uncertainty (Model Uncertainty)

Analogy: You're trying to fit a curve through a few data points. With only 3 points, there are many possible curves. That's epistemic uncertainty — you don't know which curve is right. With 1000 points, the uncertainty shrinks.

Characteristics:

  • Reducible — it shrinks as you collect more data
  • Comes from lack of knowledge about the true weights
  • Examples: uncertainty due to small training set, uncertainty about unseen inputs
  • In BNNs: the spread of the posterior p(w|D)
Epistemic uncertainty — uncertainty about the weights
$$\operatorname{Var}_{w \sim p(w \mid D)}[y \mid x, w]$$
(= variance caused by not knowing the true weights; we sample different possible weights $w$ from the posterior $p(w \mid D)$, and see how much the prediction $y$ changes; this shrinks as we get more data)

This is what BNNs add that normal NNs lack. A normal NN gives you one set of weights. A BNN gives you a distribution over weights. The spread of that distribution tells you how uncertain the model is.

Total Predictive Uncertainty

When you make a prediction with a BNN, the total uncertainty is the sum of both types:

Total Variance = Aleatoric + Epistemic
$$\operatorname{Var}[y \mid x, D] = \color{#1565c0}{\mathbb{E}_{w \sim p(w \mid D)}[\operatorname{Var}(y \mid x, w)]} + \color{#e65100}{\operatorname{Var}_{w \sim p(w \mid D)}(\mathbb{E}[y \mid x, w])}$$
⏤ aleatoric ⏤ ⏤ epistemic ⏤
💡 Subtlety: The first term $\mathbb{E}_{w \sim p(w \mid D)}[\operatorname{Var}(y \mid x, w)]$ is labeled "aleatoric", but it averages the aleatoric variance over the posterior of $w$. This means it implicitly depends on epistemic uncertainty too — a wider posterior (more epistemic uncertainty) changes the average. The decomposition is conceptually useful but the two terms are not fully independent in practice.
💡 Why this matters: If you deploy a neural network in a hospital, you need to know when it's unsure. A normal NN will confidently predict "benign" even for a tumor that looks nothing like anything in the training data. A BNN would say "I don't know" — the epistemic uncertainty would be very high.

What BNNs Give You That Normal NNs Don't

CapabilityNormal NNBNN
Point prediction ✓ Yes ✓ Yes
Aleatoric uncertainty ✓ If trained for it ✓ Yes
Epistemic uncertainty ✗ No ✓ Yes
Out-of-distribution detection ✗ Poor ✓ Good
Calibrated probabilities ✗ Often overconfident ✓ Better calibrated
Small data performance ✗ Overfits easily ✓ More robust (with good prior)
⚠️ Important caveat: Whether these benefits materialize in practice depends heavily on which approximation method you use and how well you tune it. A poorly-tuned BNN may be worse than a well-tuned normal NN. Izmailov et al. (2021) showed that common BNN approximations are systematically misspecified — the uncertainty they produce is not perfectly calibrated.

5. MLE, Prediction Intervals, and When a BNN Is Overkill

The previous sections described BNNs as the solution for uncertainty quantification. But there are other ways to think about uncertainty — and for many practical questions, a BNN is not necessary. This section clarifies the landscape.

5.1 What Is MLE? (Maximum Likelihood Estimation)

Most neural networks are trained by maximum likelihood estimation. The idea: find the single set of weights w that makes the observed data as probable as possible. Formally:

Maximum Likelihood Estimation
$$w_{\text{MLE}} = \operatorname{argmax}_w p(D \mid w)$$

This produces a point estimate — one answer, no uncertainty. Training a neural network with mean squared error (regression) or cross-entropy (classification) is MLE under the hood.

🔍 For the mathematician: MLE is just the mode of the likelihood. Under regularity conditions, the MLE is asymptotically normal: $\sqrt{n} (\hat{w} - w_{\text{true}}) \to \mathcal{N}(0, I(w)^{-1})$, where $I(w)$ is the Fisher information. This means for large datasets, even a frequentist can construct confidence intervals around the MLE. But:
  • These are asymptotic — they only hold in the limit of infinite data
  • They quantify uncertainty about the parameters, not the predictions
  • For neural networks, the Fisher is millions × millions — impractical to compute

5.2 Prediction Uncertainty vs. Parameter Uncertainty

There is a subtle but crucial distinction:

  • Parameter uncertainty — "Which weights $w$ are plausible given the data $D$?" This is what BNNs quantify: $p(w \mid D)$.
  • Prediction uncertainty — "Given a new input $x^*$, what range of $y^*$ values should I expect?" This involves both parameter uncertainty and inherent noise.

The Bayesian predictive distribution combines both:

Bayesian Predictive Distribution
$$p(y^* \mid x^*, D) = \int p(y^* \mid x^*, w) \, p(w \mid D) \, dw$$

But if the only goal is a prediction interval ("there is a 90% chance $y^*$ falls between $a$ and $b$"), there are simpler, non-Bayesian methods that give formal guarantees without ever touching the posterior over weights.

5.3 Conformal Prediction

Key insight: Conformal prediction is a distribution-free framework that turns any model's predictions into prediction sets with finite-sample coverage guarantees. No Bayesian assumptions, no asymptotic approximations, no posterior inference.

How it works (simplified):

  1. Train a model on training data (any model — neural network, random forest, linear regression)
  2. On a separate calibration set, compute a nonconformity score for each example — e.g., for regression, the absolute residual $|y - \hat{y}|$
  3. For a new input $x^*$, compute the quantile $q$ of the calibration scores (e.g., the 90th percentile for 90% coverage)
  4. Output the prediction interval: $[\hat{y}^* - q,\; \hat{y}^* + q]$

Properties of conformal prediction:

  • Finite-sample guarantee: $P(y^* \in C(x^*)) \ge 1 - \alpha$, regardless of the model or data distribution — holds for any $n$
  • Distribution-free: No assumptions about normality, homoscedasticity, or any parametric family
  • Model-agnostic: Works on top of any prediction algorithm
  • No Bayesian inference required: Just one trained model and a calibration set

Limitations:

  • Produces intervals/sets, not full distributions
  • Requires a held-out calibration set (splits the data)
  • Does not distinguish aleatoric from epistemic uncertainty
  • Does not give parameter uncertainty — no sense of "which weights are plausible"

5.4 When to Use What

GoalRecommended MethodWhy
A single best guess MLE (standard training) Fast, well-understood, works for large data
Parameter uncertainty ("which weights are plausible?") BNN (VI, Laplace, HMC) Only BNNs give the posterior over weights
Prediction intervals with guarantees ("90% chance $y \in [a,b]$") Conformal prediction Finite-sample coverage, no Bayesian assumptions, works with any model
Full predictive distribution ("what is the entire shape of $p(y|x)$?") BNN + aleatoric model, or probabilistic machine learning Gives the complete conditional distribution, not just intervals
Model comparison ("which architecture is more plausible?") Bayesian model evidence (or approximations like ELBO) Bayesian framework naturally handles model comparison via marginal likelihood
⚠️ The bottom line: Bayesian methods (BNNs) and conformal prediction answer different questions. A BNN tells you "which weights are consistent with the data." Conformal prediction tells you "what range of outputs should I expect." The former is about the model, the latter about the prediction. Neither is universally better — the right tool depends on the question being asked.
