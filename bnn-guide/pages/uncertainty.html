4. Types of Uncertainty

🎯 In one sentence

There are two kinds of uncertainty: aleatoric (irreducible, due to noise in the data) and epistemic (reducible, due to lack of knowledge). BNNs capture epistemic uncertainty; normal NNs only capture aleatoric.

📝 Notation note: what does "y | x, w" mean?

Throughout this page you'll see expressions like Var[ y | x, w ]. The vertical bar | means "given" or "conditional on".

  • y | x, w = "the value of y, given that we know x and w"
  • Var[ y | x, w ] = "the variance of y, assuming x and w are fixed"
  • Varw ~ p(w|D)[ y | x, w ] = "the variance of y (knowing x and w), but now w itself is random — so we average over w"

The pipe | is the same notation used in conditional probability: p(y|x) means "probability of y given x". Everything to the right of | is assumed known.

Aleatoric Uncertainty (Data Noise)

Analogy: Imagine measuring the height of a random person. Even if you have the perfect measuring tool, people have different heights. There's inherent randomness in the process. That's aleatoric uncertainty.

Characteristics:

  • Irreducible — you can't eliminate it by collecting more data
  • Comes from inherent noise in the data-generating process
  • Examples: sensor noise, natural variation in measurements
  • In neural networks: the uncertainty of the output given fixed weights
Aleatoric uncertainty — noise in the data
Var[ y | x, w ]
(= variance of the prediction y, given that we know the input x and the weights w; this is the inherent noise that remains even with perfect weights)

A normal neural network can model aleatoric uncertainty by predicting a distribution (e.g., mean and variance) instead of just a point. This is called heteroscedastic regression.

Epistemic Uncertainty (Model Uncertainty)

Analogy: You're trying to fit a curve through a few data points. With only 3 points, there are many possible curves. That's epistemic uncertainty — you don't know which curve is right. With 1000 points, the uncertainty shrinks.

Characteristics:

  • Reducible — it shrinks as you collect more data
  • Comes from lack of knowledge about the true weights
  • Examples: uncertainty due to small training set, uncertainty about unseen inputs
  • In BNNs: the spread of the posterior p(w|D)
Epistemic uncertainty — uncertainty about the weights
Varw ~ p(w|D)[ y | x, w ]
(= variance caused by not knowing the true weights; we sample different possible weights w from the posterior p(w|D), and see how much the prediction y changes; this shrinks as we get more data)

This is what BNNs add that normal NNs lack. A normal NN gives you one set of weights. A BNN gives you a distribution over weights. The spread of that distribution tells you how uncertain the model is.

Total Predictive Uncertainty

When you make a prediction with a BNN, the total uncertainty is the sum of both types:

Total Variance = Aleatoric + Epistemic
Var[y | x, D] = Ew~p(w|D)[Var(y | x, w)] + Varw~p(w|D)(E[y | x, w])
⏤ aleatoric ⏤ ⏤ epistemic ⏤
💡 Subtlety: The first term Ew~p(w|D)[Var(y|x,w)] is labeled "aleatoric", but it averages the aleatoric variance over the posterior of w. This means it implicitly depends on epistemic uncertainty too — a wider posterior (more epistemic uncertainty) changes the average. The decomposition is conceptually useful but the two terms are not fully independent in practice.
💡 Why this matters: If you deploy a neural network in a hospital, you need to know when it's unsure. A normal NN will confidently predict "benign" even for a tumor that looks nothing like anything in the training data. A BNN would say "I don't know" — the epistemic uncertainty would be very high.

What BNNs Give You That Normal NNs Don't

CapabilityNormal NNBNN
Point prediction ✓ Yes ✓ Yes
Aleatoric uncertainty ✓ If trained for it ✓ Yes
Epistemic uncertainty ✗ No ✓ Yes
Out-of-distribution detection ✗ Poor ✓ Good
Calibrated probabilities ✗ Often overconfident ✓ Better calibrated
Small data performance ✗ Overfits easily ✓ More robust (with good prior)
⚠️ Important caveat: Whether these benefits materialize in practice depends heavily on which approximation method you use and how well you tune it. A poorly-tuned BNN may be worse than a well-tuned normal NN. Izmailov et al. (2021) showed that common BNN approximations are systematically misspecified — the uncertainty they produce is not perfectly calibrated.
